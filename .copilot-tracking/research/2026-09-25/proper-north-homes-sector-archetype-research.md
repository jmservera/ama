## Executive Summary

* Bottom line: Proper North Homes fits the profile of a large, merged northern regional group whose stock mostly came from council stock transfers (LSVT). At 60,000 homes it would be one of about ten English groups of that size, and larger than any group whose stock sits almost entirely in the North (the largest has about 40,000). Northern comparator medians give a modelled mix of about 86.6% general needs (about 74.0% social rent and 12.6% affordable rent), 6.0% housing for older people, 2.5% supported housing and 4.9% shared ownership. Around 1,750 right to buy leaseholders sit on top of that.
* Why this matters: The archetype explains the case-study conditions (legacy systems, spreadsheet asset data, general-needs houses, low property values, high reinvestment in existing stock). It also answers the four primer open questions with figures that can be labelled as modelled estimates rather than company facts.
* Research status: Complete for research purposes. The company-specific values stay unknown company details until the organisation confirms them.
* Confidence and uncertainty: High confidence in the sector statistics and legal rules, and medium confidence in the archetype fit. Confidence in the modelled counts is medium to low: each count comes from medians, so the actual company could differ by several percentage points, particularly on shared ownership, older persons' housing and leaseholder numbers.

## What You May Not Know

* A 60,000-home association working only in the North is larger than any real northern-concentrated group in the 2025 SDR group data (largest about 40,191 units). The most plausible explanation is a recent merger of two or more stock-transfer associations. That supports the fragmented-data narrative, and the regulator's 2025 Sector Risk Profile explicitly links fragmented systems to difficulties in mergers (W4, W16).
* No new stock transfer has happened since 2015, and the regulator stopped reporting LSVTs as a separate group in 2025. Any stock-transfer heritage is therefore mature (more than 10 years old), with transfer-era promises largely delivered and a second investment cycle due (W6, W7).
* Northern associations have lower gearing and higher interest cover than the England median (for example, North East median EBITDA-MRI 152.8% against 112.9%), but much lower property values (about GBP 38,000 to 42,000 per unit against GBP 68,000). That gives them more borrowing headroom on covenants, but less security value per home (W5).
* Shared owners and leaseholders are not simply smaller versions of tenants. Retrofit in their homes is usually an improvement, not a repair. It can be charged only where the lease allows, it needs consultation above GBP 250 per leaseholder, and it can be capped for five years after a right to buy sale. Warm Homes: Social Housing Fund Wave 3 lets non-social homes make up to 30% of an application's homes, without mandatory owner contributions (W9, W10, W11).
* Official surveys suggest about three in five social renting households include someone with a long-term illness or disability. No national benchmark exists for how completely landlords record vulnerability, and the Housing Ombudsman found information-management failings in about two-thirds of upheld cases it reviewed (W12, W13).
* A web-search summary reported housing association fuel poverty at 10.0%. The primary DESNZ table shows 11.6% for 2024; the 10.0% figure was an older, superseded measure (W15).

## Findings

### Q1: English housing associations fall into a small number of recognisable archetypes

The sector is highly concentrated. There are 1,353 private registered providers, but only 8 own more than 50,000 social units and 72 own between 10,001 and 50,000 (W1 Table 1.19). At group level, 10 groups own at least 50,000 social units and 32 own at least 30,000 (W4). The recurring archetypes are:

### Q4: Lease terms usually leave retrofit in shared ownership and leasehold homes outside the landlord's direct control

Shared ownership:

* Shared owners pay for repairs and maintenance regardless of their share. In flats, the landlord arranges external and structural repairs and recovers the cost through service charges or a reserve fund (W8).
* Leases on the 2021 model include a 10-year initial repair period. In that period, the landlord covers essential external and structural repairs and up to GBP 500 a year for fixtures such as heating (W8). This helps with like-for-like heating failures, but does not fund energy upgrades.
* Improvements are the shared owner's matter, and structural changes need the landlord's permission (W8). Insulation, heat pumps or solar panels on a shared ownership house therefore depend on the owner's consent and funding, unless the landlord offers them.
* In blocks, communal fabric works fall to the landlord, and cost recovery depends on whether the lease allows improvement charges as well as repair charges.

Leasehold (right to buy and preserved right to buy):

* Leaseholders generally own the interior, while the landlord keeps the structure, exterior and common parts. Retrofit costs can be recovered through service charges only where the lease allows improvements (W17 secondary; C6 consultation evidence on lease restrictions).
* For five years after a right to buy lease is granted, repair charges are capped at the section 125 notice estimates plus inflation. Improvement contributions are limited to works estimated in that notice (Housing Act 1985 Schedule 6 paragraphs 16B and 16C) (W9).
* Section 20 consultation applies where any leaseholder's contribution to qualifying works exceeds GBP 250, or GBP 100 a year under a qualifying long-term agreement (W10). Multi-year retrofit framework contracts can therefore trigger long-term agreement consultation.
* How the social rented sector minimum energy efficiency standard (SRS MEES) will treat leasehold homes in mixed-tenure blocks is unresolved. Respondents to the 2026 government response reported cost-recovery, lease-restriction and section 20 problems; the government plans further guidance and a third-party consent exemption (C6).
* Warm Homes: Social Housing Fund Wave 3 lets non-social homes, including leaseholder and shared ownership homes, make up to 30% of an application's homes. This applies only where social homes would otherwise be adversely affected, covers insulation, ventilation and communal low-carbon heating, and needs at least 50% co-funding. Owner contributions are voluntary (W11).

* Questions: Q4
* Evidence state: evidence-backed finding for the statutory rules (W9, W10) and grant rules (W11); partially supported claim for typical lease allocation, because individual leases vary and are an unknown company detail. W11 is the Greater Manchester Combined Authority (GMCA) version of the scheme guidance; the national DESNZ guidance was not separately checked.
* Evidence: C6, W8, W9, W10, W11, W17.
* Confidence and limits: High on the rules, medium on how they apply. Applying the preserved right to buy (Housing Act 1985 sections 171A to 171H) to transferred stock was not fetched and needs checking. Actual allocation needs a lease-by-lease review of about 2,940 shared ownership and about 1,750 leasehold interests. This is internal interpretation, subject to legal review.

The number of rented households excludes the modelled 2,940 shared ownership homes and assumes one household per occupied home.

On data completeness:

* No national statistic exists for the share of residents that landlords record as vulnerable, or for the completeness of those records. The Housing Ombudsman found information-management failings in about two-thirds of upheld cases, including missed vulnerabilities. It recommended reviewing how vulnerabilities, both temporary and permanent, are recorded (W12).
* Awaab's Law guidance expects landlords to record details that indicate vulnerability to hazards. The Consumer Standards require relevant data on tenants' diverse needs (C7, C8).
* The 2025 Sector Risk Profile names fragmented and outdated systems and manual data entry as data-integrity risks (W16).

* Questions: Q5
* Evidence state: evidence-backed finding for prevalence, which is national rather than company-specific. The completeness gap is an unverified hypothesis: the recorded vulnerable share at the company is an unknown company detail and probably well below 61%.
* Evidence: C7, C8, W12, W13, W14, W15, W16.
* Confidence and limits: Medium. "Vulnerable" has no single definition. Northern incomes are lower than the national average, but housing association fuel poverty (11.6%) is below the council rate (15.7%). The smallest test is to compare the company's recorded flags with these survey benchmarks by tenure and scheme type.

### Q6: Boards typically protect safety and covenants first, fund existing homes next, and flex new supply

A sector-informed prioritisation order:

1. Statutory safety and compliance, the Decent Homes Standard and Awaab's Law. The regulator requires reliable, physically surveyed data on every home and board assurance (W16 paragraphs 2.3 to 2.7).
2. Financial covenant and golden-rule headroom. Sector EBITDA-MRI interest cover fell to 91% in 2024-25 and is not forecast to exceed 100% until 2027-28 (W16 paragraph 5.4).
3. Existing-stock investment, including reaching EPC C by 1 April 2030. The SRS MEES response allows a GBP 10,000 per-property spend exemption and a phased 2030 to 2039 regime (C6). Grant is capped at an average of GBP 7,500 per home with at least 50% co-funding (C5, W11).
4. New supply as the flexible lever. In 2025, capital spend on existing homes rose 15% to GBP 3.8 billion, while new-home investment fell 4% to GBP 11 billion (W5). The regulator notes that landlords with significant existing-stock needs "may need to manage their capacity carefully to balance investment priorities" (W16 paragraph 3.3).

For a northern stock-transfer-heritage group, the lower gearing and higher interest cover of northern medians (W5) suggest more covenant headroom than average. Low property values limit how much can be borrowed against security, and a merged group is likely to be harmonising investment standards across legacy stock.

* Questions: Q6
* Evidence state: evidence-backed finding for sector direction (W5, W16). The specific board order is a sector-informed hypothesis; the company's board policy is an unknown company detail.
* Evidence: C5, C6, W5, W11, W16.
* Confidence and limits: Medium. The regulator leaves trade-offs to boards, so the order can differ, especially where grant windows or development commitments dominate.

## Recommendation and Alternatives

* Recommendation or decision state: Model Proper North Homes as a large merged northern regional group with majority stock-transfer heritage. Use the northern pooled comparator mix (Q3), northern regional financial medians (Q2) and survey-based vulnerability prevalence (Q5), and label every value as a modelled estimate.
* Rationale: This is the only archetype consistent with 60,000 homes, a North of England footprint, general-needs dominance and legacy data fragmentation (C1, C2, W4, W6, W16).
* What could change this result: Confirmation that the organisation is part of a national group, or company-confirmed tenure, lease or board data.
