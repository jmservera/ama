## Purpose

This primer records general knowledge about UK housing associations so that
research, role agents and solution proposals start from an understanding of the
customer. Proper North Homes, the 60,000-home North of England association in
[Case Study 48](../../../case_study_48_original.md), is one such organisation.

The primer describes the sector, not Proper North Homes. Sector patterns are
not facts about the company until confirmed. Use it alongside
[Company Profile Research](../company/company-profile-research.md) and

## What a Housing Association Is

According to the source, a housing association has these defining features:

* It is an independent, not-for-profit organisation.
* It provides affordable homes to people who cannot buy or rent on the open
  market, through rent or shared ownership.
* It manages and maintains its homes to keep a good standard of living for
  tenants.
* It supports community development and provides tenant support to vulnerable
  people.
* It pursues a community-focused mission rather than a profit motive.
* It reinvests any surplus in the organisation and the environments it
  manages, rather than distributing profit.
* It is heavily regulated and often receives government support to develop new
  homes and maintain existing ones.

In England, housing associations that are registered with the Regulator of
Social Housing are private registered providers of social housing. The
regulatory duties that apply to Proper North Homes are recorded in
[Company Profile Research](../company/company-profile-research.md) and in the
[reference register](../../regulatory/README.md).

## Tenure Types

The source identifies three main types of home offered by housing
associations. The last three rows are added from official statistics because
the source does not cover them:

| Tenure | Source description | Verification note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social rent | For people on the lowest incomes, often around 50% of market rates and subsidised by government to stay affordable | The 50% figure is the source's approximation. Social rents are set under the regulator's Rent Standard and vary by area and property |
| Affordable rent | Rent capped at 80% of market value including service charges, for people who cannot afford open-market rent but do not meet social rent criteria | Consistent with the 80% inclusive cap in the Rent Standard. Confirm the current standard before use |
| Shared ownership | The buyer purchases a share of the home and pays rent to the association on the share they do not own | Lease terms determine the association's and the shared owner's repair and improvement responsibilities. See [Retrofit Responsibility in Shared Ownership and Leasehold Homes](#retrofit-responsibility-in-shared-ownership-and-leasehold-homes) |
| Supported housing | Not covered by the source. Homes let with care, support or supervision for residents with specific needs | RSH reports supported housing and housing for older people together (stock and rents 2024 to 2025, Table 1.1) |
| Housing for older people | Not covered by the source. Homes designed or designated for older residents, sometimes with a warden or alarm service | Reported with supported housing in RSH Table 1.1 |
| Non-social leasehold | Not covered by the source. Homes sold on long leases, mostly through right to buy or preserved right to buy, where the association keeps the freehold | Counted separately from social units: about 95,900 homes, up 91% since 2015 (RSH 2025 PRP Stock Briefing Note) |

The England private registered provider social stock mix is 65.2% general
needs social rent, 12.0% general needs affordable rent, 12.9% supported
housing and housing for older people, 0.6% affordable rent supported housing,
and 9.3% shared ownership (RSH, Registered provider social housing stock and
rents in England 2024 to 2025, Table 1.1). These are national figures.
Northern-concentrated groups typically hold more general needs stock and less
shared ownership (RSH group-level SDR tool 2025).

## Retrofit Responsibility in Shared Ownership and Leasehold Homes

Retrofit in shared ownership and leasehold homes is usually an improvement,
not a repair, and the association does not control it in the way it controls
works to tenanted homes. The allocation of responsibility below is internal
interpretation of the cited rules, subject to legal review. Individual leases
vary, and actual responsibility can only be confirmed by reading the lease.

### Shared Ownership

* Shared owners pay for repairs and maintenance regardless of the share they
  own. In flats, the landlord arranges external and structural repairs and
  recovers the cost through service charges or a reserve fund.
* Leases on the 2021 model include a 10-year initial repair period, in which
  the landlord covers essential external and structural repairs and up to
  GBP 500 a year for fixtures such as heating. This helps with like-for-like
  heating failures but does not fund energy upgrades.
* Improvements are the shared owner's matter, and structural changes need the
  landlord's permission. Insulation, heat pumps or solar panels on a shared
  ownership house therefore depend on the owner's consent and funding, unless
  the landlord offers them.
* In blocks, communal fabric works fall to the landlord. Cost recovery depends
  on whether the lease allows improvement charges as well as repair charges.

Source: GOV.UK, Shared ownership: repairs and home improvements (official
guidance).

### Right to Buy and Preserved Right to Buy Leasehold

* Leaseholders generally own the interior, while the landlord keeps the
  structure, exterior and common parts. Retrofit costs can be recovered
  through service charges only where the lease allows improvements. This
  point rests on secondary guidance (London Councils Retrofit London
  leasehold guidance, Shelter and Leasehold Knowledge Partnership) and on the
  lease-restriction evidence in the SRS MEES government response.
* For five years after a right to buy lease is granted, repair charges are
  capped at the section 125 notice estimates plus inflation, and improvement
  contributions are limited to works estimated in that notice (Housing Act
  1985, Schedule 6, paragraphs 16B and 16C; legislation).
* Section 20 consultation applies where any leaseholder's contribution to
  qualifying works exceeds GBP 250, or GBP 100 a year under a qualifying
  long-term agreement (Service Charges (Consultation Requirements) (England)
  Regulations 2003, SI 2003/1987, regulations 4 and 6; legislation).
  Multi-year retrofit framework contracts can therefore trigger long-term
  agreement consultation.
* How the social rented sector minimum energy efficiency standard (SRS MEES)
  will treat leasehold homes in mixed-tenure blocks is unresolved. Respondents
  reported cost-recovery, lease-restriction and section 20 problems, and the
  government said clear guidance on the respective responsibilities of
  providers and owner-occupier leaseholders will be needed. Source:
  [SRS MEES government response 2026](../../regulatory/energy-efficiency/social-rented-sector-mees-government-response-2026.md),
  `## Leasehold` and its `### Government response`, converted 2026-09-19.
  This is final policy, not by itself evidence of a commenced landlord duty.
* The preserved right to buy provisions for transferred stock (Housing Act
  1985, sections 171A to 171H) have not yet been checked. Do not apply the
  five-year cap to preserved right to buy leases until they are.

### Grant Treatment of Non-Social Homes

Warm Homes: Social Housing Fund Wave 3 lets non-social homes, including
leaseholder and shared ownership homes, make up to 30% of an application's
homes. This applies only where social homes would otherwise be adversely
affected, covers insulation, ventilation and communal low-carbon heating, and
needs at least 50% co-funding. Owner contributions are voluntary. This is a
funding condition, verified only in the Greater Manchester Combined Authority
(GMCA) copy of the Wave 3 scheme guidance (version 1, section 2.4.2). The
national DESNZ guidance has not been checked against it.

## Funding Model

The source describes housing associations as non-profit bodies that often
receive government support for new development and maintenance of existing
homes. Surpluses are reinvested rather than paid out.

The source does not describe borrowing, grant conditions or financial
regulation. Those topics are covered in the grant and financial sections of
[UK Social Housing Baseline Research](./uk-social-housing-baseline-research.md).
The sector pattern for balancing safety, covenants, existing homes and new
supply is described in [How Boards Prioritise Capital](#how-boards-prioritise-capital).

The source ranks L&Q second and Peabody third, although its own figures place

The source states that government targets include 1.5 million homes, of which

years. GOV.UK guidance for the Social and Affordable Homes Programme 2026 to
2036 describes a ten-year programme from April 2026 to March 2036 supporting

guidance, not the source's five-year framing.

Stock transfer shaped much of the northern sector. From 1988 to 2016,
1,317,176 council homes moved to housing associations in 306 transactions
(UK Housing Review 2019, Table 68a, a secondary compilation of Homes England
data). No new stock transfer has taken place since 2015, and the regulator
stopped reporting large-scale voluntary transfer (LSVT) providers as a
separate segment in 2025 (RSH, Value for money metrics and reporting 2025,
paragraph 3.22). Any stock-transfer heritage is therefore more than 10 years
old.

## Common Housing Association Archetypes

The archetypes below are an analytical grouping, not a regulatory
classification. The regulator no longer publishes archetype segments, and
they overlap: a group can be both stock-transfer heritage and merged.

| Archetype | Typical characteristics | Northern relevance | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stock-transfer (LSVT) heritage association | Formed from council stock. General needs dominated, houses and low-rise flats, right to buy and preserved right to buy leaseholders, high reinvestment in existing homes | Very common; many northern providers have this origin | RSH VfM metrics 2025, paragraph 3.22; UK Housing Review 2019, Table 68a |
| Merged regional group | Two or more associations combined to reach 30,000 or more homes; legacy systems and policies harmonised over several years | Most large northern providers | RSH group-level SDR tool 2025; RSH Sector Risk Profile 2025 |
| National multi-region group | 50,000 to 100,000 homes across several regions, with larger development and commercial arms | Some national groups hold 28% to 64% of their stock in the North | RSH group-level SDR tool 2025 |
| Traditional developing association | Grew through grant-funded development since the 1970s and 1980s; mixed tenure, more affordable rent and shared ownership | Present, usually smaller | RSH stock and rents 2024 to 2025; RSH VfM metrics 2025 |
| Specialist supported or older persons' provider | 30% or more supported or older persons' housing, with care and support contracts | Present but distinct | RSH group-level SDR tool 2025 |
| London and South East large developer | High shared ownership and market sale exposure; high building-safety spend on flats | Not applicable | RSH VfM metrics 2025; RSH Sector Risk Profile 2025 |
| For-profit and lease-based providers | Newer providers, often focused on shared ownership or leased supported housing | Limited | RSH 2025 PRP Stock Briefing Note; RSH Sector Risk Profile 2025 |
| Small community or almshouse bodies | Most providers by count, but a small share of homes | Limited | RSH stock and rents 2024 to 2025, Additional Table 1.19 |

National survey evidence describes social renters in England. The figures
cover all social renters, including council tenants, not only housing

| Indicator | Rate | Survey year | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Household member with a long-term illness or disability | 61% | 2024 to 2025 | English Housing Survey (EHS) 2024 to 2025, headline findings on demographics and household resilience, Chapter 1 |
| Household reference person aged 65 or over | 28% | 2024 to 2025 | EHS 2024 to 2025, demographics, Chapter 1 |
| Household in the lowest income quintile | 50% | 2024 to 2025 | EHS 2024 to 2025, demographics, Chapter 1 |
| Receiving housing support (Housing Benefit or the Universal Credit housing element) | 63% | 2023 to 2024 | EHS 2023 to 2024, rented sectors, Chapter 1.5 |
| Housing association households in fuel poverty, low income low energy efficiency (LILEE) measure | 11.6% | 2024 | DESNZ Annual Fuel Poverty Statistics 2025, via UK Housing Review 2026, Table 39a |

A housing association fuel poverty figure of 10.0% also circulates. It comes
from the older low income high costs (LIHC) measure and is superseded; do not
use it as current. Under LILEE, housing association fuel poverty (11.6%) is
below the council rate (15.7%).

Recorded vulnerability data is a separate question from prevalence:

* No national statistic exists for the share of residents that landlords
  record as vulnerable, or for how complete those records are.
* In May 2023 the Housing Ombudsman found information-management failings in
  about two-thirds of the upheld cases it reviewed, including missed
  vulnerabilities, and recommended that landlords review how they record
  temporary and permanent vulnerabilities.
* Awaab's Law guidance expects landlords to record details that indicate a
  resident's vulnerability to hazards. See the
  [Phase 1 guidance](../../regulatory/social-housing-regulation/awaabs-law-phase-1-guidance-2025.md)
  and the
  [Phase 2 guidance](../../regulatory/social-housing-regulation/awaabs-law-phase-2-guidance-2026.md),
  both official non-statutory guidance converted on 2026-09-19. The Consumer
  Standards also require relevant data on tenants' diverse needs.
* The regulator's Sector Risk Profile 2025 names fragmented and outdated
  systems and manual data entry as data-integrity risks.

"Vulnerable" has no single definition. Survey prevalence is a benchmark for
testing a landlord's recorded flags, not an estimate of what a landlord has
recorded.

## How Boards Prioritise Capital

The order below is a sector-informed hypothesis drawn from regulator
publications and sector finance data. The regulator leaves trade-offs to each
board, so a board's actual order can differ, especially where grant windows or
development commitments dominate.

1. Statutory safety and compliance, the Decent Homes Standard and Awaab's Law.
   The regulator expects reliable, physically surveyed data on every home and
   board assurance (RSH Sector Risk Profile 2025, paragraphs 2.3 to 2.7).
2. Financial covenant and golden-rule headroom. The Sector Risk Profile 2025
   reports sector EBITDA-MRI interest cover of 91% for 2024-25, not forecast
   to exceed 100% until 2027-28 (paragraph 5.4).
3. Existing-stock investment, including reaching EPC C. The
   [SRS MEES government response 2026](../../regulatory/energy-efficiency/social-rented-sector-mees-government-response-2026.md)
   (final policy, converted 2026-09-19) requires EPC C on one reformed-EPC
   metric by 1 April 2030 and allows a GBP 10,000 per-property spend exemption
   (`### Compliance date and metrics` and `### MEES Specific Spend
   Exemptions`). Warm Homes: Social Housing Fund Wave 3 grant is capped at an
   average of GBP 7,500 per home with at least 50% co-funding. The grant cap
   and the spend exemption measure different things: one limits grant per
   home, the other limits the spend required before an exemption applies.
4. New supply as the flexible lever. In 2025, capital spend on existing homes
   rose 15% to GBP 3.8 billion, while investment in new homes fell 4% to
   GBP 11 billion (RSH, Value for money metrics and reporting 2025). The
   regulator notes that landlords with significant existing-stock needs "may
   need to manage their capacity carefully to balance investment priorities"
   (Sector Risk Profile 2025, paragraph 3.3).

Regional finance medians differ from the national picture. Northern providers
have lower gearing and higher interest cover than the England median (for
example, a North East median EBITDA-MRI of 152.8% against 112.9% for
England), but much lower property values, at about GBP 38,000 to 42,000 per
unit against GBP 68,000 (RSH VfM metrics 2025, regional medians). That gives
more covenant headroom but less security value per home.

The 91% interest cover figure is the regulator's sector-level figure in the
Sector Risk Profile 2025.
[UK Social Housing Baseline Research](./uk-social-housing-baseline-research.md)
reports 87% as the weighted sector average in the 2025 Global Accounts. The
two figures probably use different bases and have not been reconciled; cite
the source and basis with either figure.

## Implications for Proper North Homes

The following points are internal interpretation and sector-informed
hypotheses. They are not validated facts about Proper North Homes.

* Retrofit investment competes with new supply, repairs, safety and
  regeneration for reinvested surplus and borrowing capacity. This reinforces
  the competing capital priorities described in
  [Company Profile Research](../company/company-profile-research.md). See
  [How Boards Prioritise Capital](#how-boards-prioritise-capital) for the
  sector pattern.
* Rent controls on social and affordable rent limit the scope to recover
  retrofit cost through rent. Resident benefit is more likely to appear as
  lower energy bills, warmer homes and fewer damp and mould hazards.
* A mixed tenure profile may change the consent, cost responsibility and
  communication route for each home. Shared owners and leaseholders are likely
  to need a different route from assured tenants. See
  [Tenure Types](#tenure-types) and
  [Retrofit Responsibility in Shared Ownership and Leasehold Homes](#retrofit-responsibility-in-shared-ownership-and-leasehold-homes).
* The tenant-support mission means a meaningful share of residents may be
  vulnerable. Consent, access and communication agents need vulnerability
  safeguards and human escalation by design. See
  [Residents and Vulnerability Data](#residents-and-vulnerability-data).
* A not-for-profit, regulated organisation will expect transparent governance,
  auditable decisions and board-level accountability for agent-assisted work.

## Evidence Limitations

The primer started from a short consumer-facing article from a property portal
that also promotes shared ownership listings. The archetype, tenure, lease,
resident and board sections now rest on official statistics, legislation,
guidance and regulator publications, but these limits remain:

* Sector counts and survey rates are national or regional. They describe the
  sector, not any one association, and the survey rates include council
  tenants.
* Modelled values for Proper North Homes, held in company research, come from
  comparator medians and national rates and can differ from the company by
  several percentage points.
* Northern regional shares from the RSH combined data tool are unweighted, so
  they are indicative only.
* The 30% non-social rule for Warm Homes: Social Housing Fund Wave 3 was
  verified only in the GMCA copy of the scheme guidance.
* The preserved right to buy provisions (Housing Act 1985, sections 171A to
  171H) have not been checked.
* Several legislation and guidance sources have no local snapshot. Check them
  at the official source before relying on them.

Figures and targets may change without notice. Refresh this primer when an
official or sector-body source provides a stronger account of the same
topics.
